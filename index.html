Pitch Shaper

R Shiny app. Statcast, LightGBM, physics-informed movement model.

Open the app View the code

The app can take up to a minute to wake up on its first load.

This RShiny App allows users to explore how individual spin and release traits actually contribute to IVB and HB, as well as the overall graded quality of the pitch. With the original version of this app that’s used internally by Orioles Scouting, users can select amateur pitchers (rather than MLB pitchers), automatically setting each input to that player’s own spin/release traits (adjusted based on how we expect the traits to change with the transition to flat-seamed MLB baseballs), allowing scouts to ask and answer questions such as:

  • We project this 18-year-old to add 3 mph over the next five years. How does his full arsenal grade if he does?
  • If we can get him to drop his arm slot a few degrees, how much fastball run would he gain, and how much ride would he sacrifice?
  • If he swaps his circle-change for a kick-change, losing the expected 600 RPM and shifting his spin axis, how does the shape change?
  • Once his traits are adjusted to the MLB ball, is his slider still a plus pitch?
  • Player Development thinks his low training age leaves room for 2 more inches of extension. How much does that improve his fastball grade?
  • This kid’s sweeper shows more IVB than his spin traits predict. Are seam effects working against him?
  • Etc.

Several layers of modeling are integrated on the back-end, built on ~3 million rows of publicly available Statcast data: one to create the pitch grader’s output feature (expected run value based on batted-ball data), the pitch grader itself, controlling for pitch location, count, platoon, and batter quality, and a physics-informed model for predicting expected movement based on spin/release traits, which is an innately formulaic system (the only missing piece of critical information is pitch grip/seam effects, which I hope to add eventually). It should be noted that the pitch grader isn’t quite as advanced as it could be, I chose to exclude some features for the sake of compatibility with the expected movement model, such as break velocity, pitch sequencing, and pitch-to-pitch variance.

Preference List Review

R Shiny app. Adaptive head-to-head ranking over a scouting preference list.

Open the app View the code

The app can take up to a minute to wake up on its first load.

This tool was used by Orioles scouts leading up to the Rule 4 Draft to refine their preference lists, allowing for more easily digestible head-to-head player comparisons, automatically adjusting internal preference lists. This recreated version includes follow reports I’ve written on high school players, allowing you to explore my evaluation/report writing abilities. If you’re familiar with the prospects, though, feel free to complete the review according to your opinions, and you can see where our evaluations differ. Please forgive my excessive conciseness/abbreviating, I’m sure your scouts encounter the same character-count limitations when writing reports.

Optimizing the Bonus Pool

R, Stan and C++. Bayesian value curves, a calibrated draft-market simulator and a decision engine for the Rule 4 bonus pool.

Read the report View the code

One conclusion I drew from working in an MLB draft room this July is that teams across the league are leaving copious value on the table by not being more statistically rigorous in how they choose to spend the bonus pool. In this independent project, I built a staged R + Stan pipeline that seeks to refine this process. The pipeline estimates what a draftee is worth as a function of the bonus he signs for, models how the draft market prices and sorts players, simulates thousands of drafts, and searches for allocation rules inside that simulated market. It's built entirely from public data on the 2012–25 drafts, and answers questions like:

  • Should we save money at our first pick, and if so, how much?
  • What is $1M of pool space worth when we move it from the first round to day two, or past round 10?
  • How far into the overage tax should we plan before a failed signing costs us a future first?
  • How much does signing risk change the plan for a high schooler who fell?
  • How much of any edge comes from how the market prices players, and how much from the rules themselves?

Under the hood: a mixture cure model for MLB arrival that uses early milestones (level reached, Top 100 appearances), a censored ordinal model for value over the six-year control window, with still-open windows filled in by a season-level accrual model, and Bayesian models for deal structure, where ranked prospects come off the board, and signing risk. Those feed a draft-market simulator calibrated to 2014–25 drafts, and a C++ engine that tests allocation rules across 2,000 simulated drafts and 100 posterior draws at 13 first-round positions.

The headline result: a rule that re-prices the board at every pick (each player's expected value at his ask, minus a shadow price on pool dollars) beats best-available-at-slot by $8–14M of expected class surplus at every first-round position, while every fixed "save at the top" or "spend at the top" template loses to it. The report walks through the methods, the code that matters, the model checks, and where the simulator still misses.
